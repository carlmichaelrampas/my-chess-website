The board, the pieces and the rules

Everything in chess grows out of a handful of rules. Learn these once and you can read any game — or play one — without guessing.

On this page

  1. The board
  2. The pieces
  3. Three special moves
  4. How a game ends
  5. Piece values

The board

Chess is played on an 8×8 board of 64 squares, alternating light and dark. The eight vertical columns are called files and are lettered a to h from White's left to right. The eight horizontal rows are ranks, numbered 1 to 8 from White's side. Every square has a name: the bottom-left corner from White's view is a1, and the square four files and four ranks in is e4.

Set the board so that each player has a light square on their right. White's pieces start on ranks 1 and 2, Black's on ranks 7 and 8. The queens begin on their own colour: the white queen on d1 (light), the black queen on d8 (dark).

White always moves first, and players then take turns — one move each, no passing.

The pieces

♞ King One square in any direction
♛ Queen Any distance: file, rank or diagonal
♜ Rook Any distance: file or rank
♝ Bishop Any distance, diagonally
♞ Knight In an L: two squares then one
♟ Pawn One forward, captures diagonally

How each piece moves

  • King — one square in any direction. The king may never move onto a square attacked by an enemy piece.
  • Queen — any number of empty squares along a file, rank or diagonal. The most powerful piece.
  • Rook — any number of empty squares along its file or rank. Very strong on open files.
  • Bishop — any number of empty squares diagonally. A bishop stays on the colour it started on for the whole game.
  • Knight — an L-shape: two squares in one direction and one square sideways. It is the only piece that jumps over others.
  • Pawn — one square forward, or two from its starting square. Pawns capture one square diagonally forward, never straight ahead.
No two pieces on one square A piece captures by moving onto an enemy piece's square, removing it from the board. You can never capture your own pieces, and you cannot jump over pieces — except with the knight.

Three special moves

1. Castling

Once per game, the king and a rook can move together. The king slides two squares toward the rook and the rook jumps to the square on the king's other side. Kingside castling is written O-O (king to g1), queenside is O-O-O (king to c1).

Castling is only legal when:

  • neither the king nor that rook has moved yet;
  • every square between them is empty;
  • the king is not in check, and does not pass through or land on an attacked square.

2. En passant

If an enemy pawn moves two squares and lands directly beside your pawn, you may capture it as if it had only moved one square — your pawn moves diagonally forward and the enemy pawn is removed. This is only allowed on your very next move; after that the chance is gone.

3. Promotion

A pawn that reaches the far rank is promoted: it becomes a queen, rook, bishop or knight of its own colour. Most promotions are to a queen, but a knight can occasionally be stronger. You can have more than one queen.

How a game ends

  • Check — the king is attacked. You must answer it: move the king, block the attack, or capture the attacking piece.
  • Checkmate — the king is in check and there is no legal way out. The game ends immediately; the side delivering mate wins.
  • Resignation — a player may give up at any time.
  • Stalemate — the player to move is not in check but has no legal move. That is a draw.

There are also several ways to draw a game:

  • Agreement — both players accept a draw.
  • Fifty-move rule — 50 moves each with no capture and no pawn move.
  • Threefold repetition — the same position occurs three times.
  • Insufficient material — neither side has enough material to mate, such as king and bishop alone against a bare king.
Checkmate ends the game — you do not capture the king Once the king cannot escape a check, the game stops. The king is never actually taken off the board.

Piece values

These numbers are a rough guide to how much material a piece is worth. They help you decide whether a trade is good.

PieceValueNotes
Pawn1The unit of measurement. Worth more as it nears promotion.
Knight3Strongest in the centre; loves closed positions.
Bishop3Strong on long open diagonals.
Rook5Powerful on open files and the 7th rank.
Queen9Worth roughly a rook and a bishop.
King—Priceless: the game is over if it is checkmated.
The two bishops are worth more than two knights On an open board, a bishop pair (about 6) cooperates better than two knights (also about 6) because the bishops cover long lines together. Values are a guide, not a law — position matters more.
