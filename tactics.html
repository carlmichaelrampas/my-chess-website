Tactics: the patterns that win games

Most games between improving players are decided by a two- or three-move combination, not by deep strategy. These are the patterns behind almost all of them.

  2. Fork
  3. Pin

How to look for tactics

On every move, run the same short checklist: checks, captures, threats. First look at every check you can give (yours and your opponent's), then every capture, then every direct threat. Most tactics are hiding in one of those three categories.

Before you capture, count Add up the value of the pieces attacking and defending a square. If your capture wins a rook (5) but loses a bishop (3), it was not a good trade — even if it looked aggressive.

Fork

One piece attacks two targets at once. Knights are the classic forking piece, because their awkward movement is hard to see.

Example. A white knight on c7 attacks both the black king on a8 and the rook on e8. Black must answer the check, and the knight takes the rook next move. A knight landing on c7 or f7 is a recurring theme in real games.

Forks are not only for knights: a queen can fork a king and a loose piece, and pawn forks (attacking two pieces with a pawn push) are especially sweet because a pawn is worth so little.

Pin

A pinned piece cannot move without exposing something more valuable behind it. There are two kinds:

  • Absolute pin — the king is behind the pinned piece, so moving it would be illegal.
  • Relative pin — a valuable piece (often the queen) is behind it. Moving is legal, but usually bad.

Example. A white bishop on b5 pins the black knight on c6 to the king on e8. The knight is frozen: it cannot leave the diagonal. This is why Bb5 in the Ruy López is such a natural move — it immediately creates pressure without any risk.

Skewer

The mirror image of a pin: you attack a valuable piece in front, and when it moves out of the way you capture the piece behind it.

Example. A white bishop on b2 checks the black king on g7, with a black rook sitting on h8 on the same diagonal. The king must step aside, and the bishop takes the rook.

Discovered attack and double check

A discovered attack happens when moving one piece opens a line for another. If the piece being uncovered gives check, the move is a discovered check — and the moving piece is free to grab something valuable, because the check must be answered first.

Example. A white bishop on b2 is blocked by its own knight on e5, with the black king on g7. Moving the knight anywhere exposes the bishop's check on the king.

If the moving piece also gives check, that is a double check — and it is brutal. You cannot block or capture your way out of two checks at once, so the king must move. From the same setup, jumping the knight to g6 checks the king on h8 while opening the bishop's diagonal at the same time.

Back-rank mate

A king that has castled can end up boxed in by its own pawns. If an enemy rook or queen reaches the back rank, the result is often mate.

Example. The black king is on g8 with pawns on f7, g7 and h7. A white rook arriving on a8 gives check along the eighth rank. The king cannot step to f8 or h8 (still on the rank) and cannot move forward (its own pawns are there). That is checkmate.

Prevention Make luft — a little breathing space for your king. Pushing one of the pawns in front of it, or leaving a rook on the back rank to defend, removes most back-rank tactics at once.

Quick quiz

Try to answer before opening each question.

1. White bishop on b5, black knight on c6, black king on e8. What is happening to the knight?

It is pinned — an absolute pin. Moving the knight would expose the king to the bishop, so it is stuck.

2. White knight jumps to c7. The black king is on a8 and a rook sits on e8. What is the result?

A fork. The knight checks the king on a8 and attacks the rook on e8; Black saves the king and White wins the rook.

3. Black has castled short: king on g8, pawns on f7, g7, h7. A white rook lands on a8. Why is this mate?

The rook checks along the eighth rank. The king has no escape square and cannot capture the rook, and none of Black's pieces can block or take it — a classic back-rank mate.
