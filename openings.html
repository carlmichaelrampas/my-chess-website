Openings: start with a plan

You do not need to memorise twenty moves. You need three goals and a handful of sound first moves — the rest follows from the position.

On this page

  1. The three goals
  2. Eight practical rules
  3. Six openings worth knowing
  4. Common mistakes

The three goals

Every good opening move works toward one of these:

  • Control the centre. The four squares d4, e4, d5, e5 are the high ground. Pieces placed there reach more of the board.
  • Develop your pieces. Bring knights and bishops out to active squares, and castle to tuck the king away.
  • Keep your king safe. Usually that means castling within the first ten moves, before the centre opens up.

Eight practical rules

  1. Aim a pawn at the centre on move one — e4 or d4 with White.
  2. Develop knights and bishops before you start looking for tricks.
  3. Castle early. An uncastled king is a target.
  4. Do not move the same piece twice without a reason.
  5. Do not bring the queen out early — it can be chased around while your opponent develops.
  6. Do not grab pawns at the cost of development.
  7. Connect your rooks by clearing the back rank.
  8. Ask what your opponent's last move threatens before you choose your own.
Notation in one line A move like Nf3 means "knight to f3". Pieces are K, Q, R, B and N; pawns have no letter, so e4 is a pawn move. Captures use x, check is + and checkmate is #.

Six openings worth knowing

The Italian Game 1.e4 e5

1. e4 e5  2. Nf3 Nc6  3. Bc4

The most natural way to start: both sides take the centre and develop quickly. The bishop on c4 stares at f7, the weakest square in Black's camp, and White often follows with c3 and d4 to build a big centre. Excellent for learning because the plans are easy to understand.

The Ruy López 1.e4 e5

1. e4 e5  2. Nf3 Nc6  3. Bb5

Instead of the direct Bc4, White pins the knight on c6 — the piece defending the pawn on e5 — and keeps the pressure on for a long time. One of the oldest and most respected openings; it rewards patience and small positional advantages.

The Sicilian Defence 1.e4 c5

1. e4 c5  2. Nf3 d6  3. d4 cxd4  4. Nxd4 Nf6  5. Nc3 a6

Black answers e4 with a side pawn and accepts an unbalanced position: White gets space in the centre, Black gets a half-open c-file and counterplay on the queenside. The line shown is the famous Najdorf Variation. It is the most popular reply to e4 at the top level.

The French Defence 1.e4 e6

1. e4 e6  2. d4 d5

Solid and slightly cramped. Black builds a pawn chain and strikes at the centre with c5 later, often aiming at White's d4 pawn. The trade-off is the light-squared bishop, which can be shut in behind the pawns — French players spend much of the game trying to free it.

The Queen's Gambit 1.d4 d5

1. d4 d5  2. c4

White offers a wing pawn to lure Black's d5 pawn away and dominate the centre. It is not really a gambit: the pawn is usually recovered. Black can decline with 2...e6 (the Queen's Gambit Declined) or accept with 2...dxc4, which takes careful play to hold.

The King's Indian Defence 1.d4 Nf6

1. d4 Nf6  2. c4 g6  3. Nc3 Bg7  4. e4 d6

Black lets White build a large centre and then attacks it. The bishop on g7 rakes the long diagonal, and the freeing pawn breaks are ...e5 or ...c5. Dynamic and double-edged, with opposite-side attacks in many lines.

Common mistakes

  • Hunting pawns with the queen. You gain a pawn and lose three tempi — usually not worth it.
  • Moving the same piece twice. Each extra move with one piece leaves another piece asleep on its starting square.
  • Delaying castling. The centre can open at any moment; a king stuck in the middle is in real danger.
  • Memorising without understanding. Ten moves you understand beat thirty you don't.
A simple plan for your first year Choose one opening for White and one for Black and play them almost every game. You will learn the typical middlegame plans, and your results will improve faster than from constantly switching.
